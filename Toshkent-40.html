Toshkent-40

All of Uzbekistan moved inside the real boundary of Tashkent: 39.5 million people today, and about 53 million by the time the last spire is finished in 2052, because the country keeps growing while the city is built. The plan holds 54.6 million, about 126,000 people per km², four and a half times Manhattan. Build tall enough in the right places and more than a quarter of the city still stays parkland.

01 · Where 54.6 million people sleep

Four ways of living, sized for 2052

The plan budgets 55 m² of floor space per person for everything: home, work, school, clinic, shops, corridors and lift shafts. Housing comes in four forms, so people can choose between a sky apartment, a tower flat, a courtyard block close to the ground, or the rebuilt low-rise Old Town. The counts are sized for the population when construction ends, not for today (section 06). Section 04 shows where the jobs, schools and factories go.

02 · Land budget, 435 km²

A third for homes, a quarter for parks

Because the people go up, the ground is free for parks, canals, workplaces and streets. Housing plots cover about a third of the city, and parks, forest and the green belt more than a quarter.

    Gross density, people per km²

    Locally it is far higher: each spire puts 640,000 people on a 0.6 km² plot, over a million per km² of footprint. Manhattan and Macau figures are rounded approximations.

    03 · Keeping 55 million alive

    The systems underneath

    Most of the hard engineering is below ground or inside the buildings. These are order-of-magnitude figures for a design population of about 55 million. Turn on Underground in the model to see the networks.

    Water

    11 million m³/day · 92% recycled

    200 litres per person per day. Twin trunk mains bring fresh water from the upper Chirchiq, below the Charvak dam, to an underground water works of twelve caverns; it needs only about 0.9 million m³/day (≈ 10 m³ per second) because every district recycles its wastewater through membrane bioreactors and reverse osmosis. Three ring mains carry fresh water and three purple mains carry recycled water. Each spire has its own reservoir cavern holding 30 days of water, about 3.8 million m³. The old aryk canals stay open for shade and cooling.

    Energy

    ≈ 330 GW continuous · ≈ 2,900 TWh/yr

    About 6 kW per person, covering vertical farms, cooling, water treatment and transit. About 110 fusion units of 3 GW run in caverns 20 to 40 km outside the city line, so their own waste heat never enters Tashkent. Power arrives over four independent HVDC grids. Every facade is also a solar panel.

    Food

    30 km² quarter × 50 tiers = 1,500 km² of growing floor

    Leafy greens, vegetables and fruit come from the stacked farms of the food quarter and from 20 farm floors inside every spire. Grain, cotton, meat and dairy come from robot-run farms in the Fergana and Zarafshan valleys, which are emptied of people but not of crops, by freight maglev.

    Moving around

    ≈ 165 million trips a day · no private cars

    A metro grid with lines every kilometre puts about 430 stations within a 10-minute walk of everyone. A 35 km express maglev ring runs 80 m down under the inner spires, with 8 radial express lines under the park corridors. Inside each spire, double-deck shuttles run to sky lobbies at 300, 600 and 900 m, and local lifts go on from there. Skybridges join neighbouring spires at 300 and 600 m.

    Earthquakes

    Designed for a 1-in-2,475-year event

    The 1966 earthquake flattened much of Tashkent, so seismic design comes first. Spires stand on piles about 120 m deep into the bedrock and a raft on base isolators, with a diagrid exoskeleton and tuned mass dampers in the crown. Towers and superblocks use the same isolation at smaller scale.

    Waste

    ≈ 66,000 tonnes a day

    Pneumatic tubes in every building carry rubbish underground to plasma gasification plants, which turn it into fuel gas and an inert slag used as building aggregate. No bin lorries on the street.

    Heat

    Summer highs near 40 °C

    Shade-first streets lined with chinar plane trees, open water, pale surfaces and district cooling. The spires double as 1.2 km heat chimneys: warm air is drawn up the cores and released above the city's inversion layer.

    Safety

    Refuge floor every 25 floors · 30 days self-sufficient

    Each spire has 12 refuge floors, firefighting lifts, and skybridges that work as sideways escape routes into the next spire. Water tanks, food stores and a separate grid feed let any spire run cut off for a month.

    Jobs, schools and industry

    Jobs by sector

      Over half of it is the buildings' own cores: in a 1,200 m spire about a quarter of every floor is lifts, stairs, shafts and refuge floors. The rest gives about 22 m² per office worker, 10 m² per child at school or kindergarten and 130 m² per hospital bed. In a spire, the offices fill about 13 of its 300 floors. Plan choice

      Every school is inside the building or block its pupils live in, so no child crosses a street to get to class. That means fewer, larger schools than today's 11,118, which average about 620 pupils. Each sky courtyard, tower podium and superblock also has a kindergarten. In the model, tap the low building in the corner of any superblock courtyard.

      Industry in three rings

      Inside the city

      ≈ 1.2 M jobs · clean and quiet

      Electronics, pharmaceuticals, garment finishing, food processing, robotics and 3D-print shops on maker floors in the spires and tower podiums, and in the logistics depots on the outer plots. Materials and finished goods move by underground freight tube, so there are no lorries on the streets.

      Industrial belt, 15–60 km out

      ≈ 1.1 M jobs · six towns in the model

      Work too heavy, noisy or space-hungry for the city stays in the industrial towns of Tashkent region that already do it: chemicals and hydrogen at Chirchiq, building components at Keles, a freight port at Zangiota, food and textiles at Yangiyul, cement and glass at Ohangaron and copper at Olmaliq, plus Bekabad steel and Angren coal beyond the model's edge. Nobody lives there any more: a freight-and-commuter maglev reaches each town in 10–25 minutes. Press Industrial belt in the model to see them.

      Fixed to the ground

      ≈ 0.5 M jobs · on rotation

      Gold at Muruntau, gas at Shurtan and Mubarek, the hydro dams, and the robot-run cotton and grain farms in the Fergana and Zarafshan valleys can't move. They are automated as far as possible and run by crews on two-week rotations from homes in Tashkent, the way offshore oil platforms work.

      Built first

      2027–2030 · the city's own factory

      The Keles and Ohangaron works are the first things built. They make the floor slabs, lift cores, facade units and cement that the spires and towers are assembled from, and employ a large part of the construction workforce, which peaks at about 3 million in the 2040s.

      05 · Life inside a spire

      The vertical mahalla

      The mahalla, a neighbourhood community of a few thousand people with its own elders, committee and teahouse, is the unit Uzbek cities already run on. Each spire is 128 of them stacked up, about 5,000 people each.

      A vertical mahalla spans about two floors of a 100,000 m² floor plate, wrapped around a four-storey sky courtyard, the hovli. That courtyard holds a chaikhana, a school, a clinic, a prayer room and a playground. Every 25 floors there is a sky bazaar in the spirit of Chorsu, plus a refuge floor.

      Nobody is more than ten minutes from a garden: the hovli on their own level, the courtyard of a superblock, or a park corridor on the ground.

      07 · Building it without making anyone homeless

      Twenty-five years, in order

      The rule is that nobody leaves a home until a new one is ready. Everything starts underground, then the city rises in waves and the old city is cleared behind them. Press Watch it being built above the plan to see it happen.

        08 · Limits money cannot remove

        What breaks first

        Unlimited budget and technology still leave physics and people. These are the problems the design has to live with, ranked by how hard they are.

        1. Waste heat. Nearly every watt the city uses ends up as heat inside it. At 330 GW, that is about 760 W per m² of city, roughly four times the average sunshine falling on Tashkent. Moving the power plants, bulk farms and data centres outside the city line cuts the in-city load to about 140 GW (≈ 320 W/m²), and the spire chimneys vent the rest high up. Without that, the city would cook itself.

        2. Evacuation. Getting 640,000 people out of a 1.2 km building takes hours, not minutes. The spires must be designed to keep people safe inside, with refuge floors and sideways escape into the next spire, instead of emptying them.

        3. Single points of failure. One grid fault or one poisoned water line could reach 55 million people at once. Everything critical runs as four independent networks, and any spire can run alone for 30 days.

        4. Daylight and the human scale. Deep floor plates leave many rooms far from a window. Light wells, fibre-optic daylighting and the sky courtyards help, but life in a spire will still feel very different from a house with a garden.

        5. Growth after 2052.

        So it is possible on paper. The real lesson is the reverse of the premise: the limit on how many people a city can hold is not land, it is heat, water and how fast you can get people out when something goes wrong.
